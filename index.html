01 · Research case

Why a dynamic agro-ecological framework is needed

#

Problem statement

Agro-ecological classifications integrate physiography, soils, climate and Length of Growing Period (LGP), but the climatic and growing-period components are not necessarily stationary. Shifts in rainfall timing, temperature, soil moisture, evapotranspiration, dry spells and heat exposure can alter the functional environment experienced by crops while formal geographic boundaries remain unchanged.

#

Research gap

Annual averages, monthly summaries and fixed monsoon/Kharif/Rabi windows can hide changes in seasonal timing and reliability. A stable median season can coexist with later onset, greater year-to-year variability or altered within-season dry-spell structure.

#

Core innovation

Transform the AESR framework from a static spatial classification into an annually resolved agro-ecological state system: retain relatively stable land properties while allowing climate, moisture balance and functional growing period to vary annually.

#

Central premise

An agro-ecological region may retain its geographic identity while progressively losing climatic fidelity to the growing-period regime under which that identity was originally defined.

02 · Preliminary proof of concept

E2 · Derived preliminary evidence

Daily CHIRPS-v3 rainfall was compared between 1991–2000 and 2015–2024 across hyper-arid, dry-subhumid and perhumid pilots. R10 and R90 mark the dates when cumulative annual rainfall reaches 10% and 90%. The R10–R90 interval is a rainfall-defined active-season metric, not agronomic LGP.

Perhumid

Rainfall magnitude changed substantially while rainfall-season timing and duration remained comparatively stable.

03 · Research design

Aim, objectives and hypotheses

#

Central research question

To what extent are India’s established Agro-Ecological Sub-Regions retaining their historical climatic and growing-period identities under a changing climate, where are agro-ecological transitions emerging, and how are these changes associated with crop productivity and adaptation needs?

  1. O1

    Reconstruct dynamic seasons

    Reconstruct annual functional growing seasons and LGP-related metrics from daily environmental observations.

  2. O2

    Measure displacement and instability

    Quantify changes in onset, cessation, duration, dry spells, heat exposure, moisture persistence and seasonal reliability.

  3. O3

    Detect AESR transition hotspots

    Determine where contemporary conditions remain consistent with, approach the limits of, or depart from reference AESR climatic and growing-period envelopes.

  4. O4

    Quantify crop-season synchrony

    Assess alignment between dynamic seasonal conditions, crop calendars and observed phenology.

  5. O5

    Estimate crop productivity response

    Test whether daily-derived seasonal metrics improve explanation of detrended crop-yield anomalies and yield stability, including rainfed versus irrigated response.

  6. O6

    Identify adaptation analogues

    Identify present-day agro-ecological climate analogues for future conditions and evaluate evidence-based adaptation pathways.

H1–H2 · Methodological test

Daily-derived seasons depart meaningfully from fixed calendar seasons and improve out-of-sample explanation of crop phenology and detrended yield variability.

H3–H4 · Agricultural sensitivity

Seasonal displacement and instability reduce crop-season synchrony and increase yield variability, with stronger effects in more rainfed systems.

H5 · Spatial transition

Within-AESR transition hotspots coincide with increasing crop-productivity risk after relevant climatic, irrigation, soil, technology and spatial controls.

Benchmark

Daily-derived functional-season and crop-stage models will be benchmarked against annual averages and conventional fixed-season climate summaries.

04 · Data architecture

Reference, daily climate, validation and agricultural response

Reference

AESR framework

Official AER/AESR identity, physiography, soils, reference climate, LGP and available water capacity.

Initial evidence: E0 → harmonized E1
Tier A

Historical daily backbone

Daily precipitation, temperature, moisture and water-balance variables for long-term seasonal reconstruction.

Core period: 1981–2024; reference climatology: 1991–2020
Tier B

Earth-observation validation

Independent rainfall, vegetation phenology and soil-moisture evidence used to validate derived seasonal states.

Sensor-specific temporal resolution retained
Tier C

Agricultural response

District/crop/season yield, irrigation exposure, crop calendars and administrative crosswalks.

Primary outcome: detrended crop-yield anomaly and stability
Tier D

Future extension

Multi-model future climate scenarios for AESR drift and present-day climate-analogue analysis.

Optional extension after historical validation

M1

Daily season detection

Retain daily temporal structure and estimate onset, cessation, duration, wet/dry-spell structure and thermal/moisture conditions using transparent persistence rules and sensitivity analysis.

M2

Dynamic growing-period state

Integrate rainfall, temperature, evapotranspiration/water balance and soil-moisture persistence. Rainfall concentration alone is not treated as agronomic LGP.

M3

AESR drift and fidelity

Compare annual observed conditions with reference climatic/LGP envelopes to quantify stability, one-sided/interval departures, internal fragmentation and transition hotspots.

M4

Season–crop synchrony

Align environmental exposure with sowing, vegetative, flowering, grain-filling and harvest periods using crop calendars and EO phenology where independently available.

M5

Crop-productivity models

Model detrended yield anomalies and stability with daily seasonal metrics, irrigation interactions, soils, terrain, technology trends, fixed effects and spatial dependence.

M6

Climate analogues

Match future dynamic AESR conditions to present-day agro-ecological analogues, constrained by soil, AWC, terrain and irrigation compatibility before adaptation inference.

06 · Evidence governance

E0–E5 Evidence-Maturity Framework

Download definitions

Evidence levels describe the maturity of a specific finding, not whether the finding is favourable or adverse. A severe model-derived signal can remain E2 until independently validated.

E0

Source

Authoritative or raw input evidence before analytical transformation.

E1

Harmonized

Quality controlled, standardized and spatially/temporally harmonized evidence.

E2

Derived

Indicator estimated with a documented and reproducible analytical method.

E3

Validated

Derived result independently validated, benchmarked or confirmed.

E4

Explanatory

Robust relationship supported after relevant controls and sensitivity tests.

E5

Decision-ready

Convergent validated evidence sufficiently mature for an explicit adaptation recommendation.

Claim boundary

This project distinguishes detection, association, explanation, attribution and decision recommendation. Formal anthropogenic attribution is not inferred automatically from trend detection or crop association.
